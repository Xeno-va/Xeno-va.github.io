## Background

Despite growing global interest in decarbonizing energy, **biofuels remain
underutilized and poorly optimized**. Their high energy density and
compatibility with existing transportation infrastructure make them uniquely
suited for rapid adoption, yet current production strategies are mired in
inefficiency. Most rely on edible crops like corn — consuming arable land,
exacerbating food insecurity, and depending heavily on government subsidies to
remain economically viable.

Meanwhile, **waste lignocellulosic biomass** — agricultural residues, forestry
byproducts, and urban paper and cardboard waste — accumulates globally with
limited productive use. Though technically sequestering carbon while intact,
most of it is ultimately landfilled, burned, or left to decay, **releasing its
embodied carbon without extracting energy value**. Harnessing this feedstock is
a socially and ecologically efficient pathway: it diverts waste, closes carbon
loops, and bypasses food-energy tradeoffs.

Even beyond agriculture, crystalline cellulose is locked up in **textiles,
corrugated packaging, and recycled paper products**, forming an immense,
untapped source of structural carbohydrates. These highly ordered fibers resist
enzymatic attack, which limits the feasibility of widespread conversion using
current technologies.

## Context

Industrial production of glucose from cellulose has traditionally relied on
**chemical hydrolysis** — acids or high temperatures to break down polymer
chains. Effective, but energy-intensive, corrosive, and productive of
byproducts that complicate downstream fermentation. **Enzymatic hydrolysis** is
cleaner and more targeted, but remains bottlenecked by cost and efficiency,
especially on crystalline substrates.

Microbial cultures such as *Aspergillus* or *Trichoderma* have long been used in
traditional glucose production — koji fermentation, for instance — but their
native enzyme systems are tuned for **amorphous or pretreated substrates**.
Endoglucanases, which cleave internal β-1,4-glycosidic bonds, come in several
classes (GH5, GH9, GH45), but few show high activity on **highly crystalline
fibers without pretreatment**.

The challenge is not just enzyme specificity — it is systemic. Crystalline
cellulose resists attack because of its **tightly packed hydrogen-bonded
structure**, which prevents most cellulases from reaching cleavage sites. Most
industrial strains also lack the robustness or combinatorial expression of
enzymes — LMPOs, xylanases, β-glucosidases — needed for real-world
lignin-containing biomass.

Directed enzyme evolution established that enzyme function can be dramatically
enhanced by mimicking natural selection in the lab, but that approach is
protein-specific and labor-intensive. Applying **adaptive laboratory evolution
(ALE)** to whole microbial systems — mutating and selecting strains on fitness
under real substrate constraints — offers a route to evolve not one enzyme but
**entire synergistic pathways**.

LMPOs oxidatively cleave cellulose fibers. Xylanases target hemicellulose side
chains. Without evolved, integrated strains that express and regulate these
adaptively, enzymatic waste-to-glucose conversion stays out of reach.

## The plan

The approach leverages adaptive laboratory evolution, **random mutagenesis**,
and automated selection workflows to engineer strains capable of efficiently
degrading crystalline cellulose. Rather than rational design of individual
enzymes, the aim is to evolve **entire cellulolytic systems** — favoring strains
with real-world activity on crystalline substrates without extensive
pretreatment.

The project begins by exploiting **enzyme promiscuity**: random UV or chemical
mutagenesis generates large libraries of variants, some producing novel or
overexpressed enzymes with unintended but advantageous activity on difficult
substrates. These populations are then put under growth-based or reporter-linked
selection on real crystalline cellulose — Avicel, micronized paper — under
controlled, automated incubation.

To accelerate discovery and cut manual bottlenecks, we are building **custom
microfluidic platforms and low-cost imaging systems** capable of running
thousands of parallel droplet assays. Halo assays, turbidity shifts, and
fluorescence-linked reporters enable screening for endoglucanase, LMPO, and
xylanase activity at scale. Promising candidates funnel into longer ALE cycles,
where selective pressure on mixed substrates — lignin-rich agricultural waste —
drives synergistic pathway development.

The goal is not single-enzyme optimization. It is to evolve **entire microbial
systems** capable of coordinated degradation across cellulose, hemicellulose,
and lignin-rich interfaces. Using strain libraries, synthetic consortia, and
high-throughput analytics, we aim to identify and stabilize metabolic pathways
that convert a wide range of real-world biomass inputs into fermentable glucose
with minimal preprocessing.

In parallel, we are developing **cost models and downstream compatibility
frameworks** so that evolved strains and enzyme blends are deployable within
existing industrial fermentation pipelines — bioethanol, bioplastics, and
agricultural bio-inputs.

This marks the beginning of a broader platform to systematically evolve and
scale microbial tools for **circular biomanufacturing**, where waste becomes
feedstock and biology does the heavy lifting.
